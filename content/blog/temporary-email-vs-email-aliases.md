# Temporary email vs. email aliases: which one fits the task?

If you want to keep your real inbox out of a signup, both temporary email and email aliases can do it, but they solve different problems. [A temporary email address](/temporary-email) is a short-lived inbox that you let expire. An email alias is a forwarding address that stays attached to your permanent mailbox until you turn it off. The practical question is not which one is "more private," but whether you need to receive mail at that address again next week. Answer that first and the choice usually makes itself. This guide compares how each works, where each breaks down, and how to pick one without exposing an address you actually depend on.

## The core difference: expiry versus forwarding

The simplest way to separate the two is to ask what happens to the address over time. A temporary inbox is designed to disappear. You create it, receive the message you need, and the address reaches the end of its configured lifetime. On the deployment described here, the operator sets a default retention of 60 minutes and a maximum of 1440 minutes, so the countdown you see is the authoritative expiry for your inbox, not a fixed product guarantee that applies everywhere. Once that window closes, access ends and the address cannot be reused to receive new mail. An alias has no countdown at all. It forwards everything to a mailbox you already control and keeps working until you deliberately disable or delete it. That makes an alias a long-term privacy filter and a temporary inbox a short-term receiving point. Neither is inherently better; they map to different lengths of commitment, which is why the lifetime of the relationship should drive the decision.

## How email aliases actually work

An alias is an address that forwards. When a website sends mail to the alias, the provider relays it into your real mailbox, so the sender never sees your primary address. Mozilla's Firefox Relay describes these as "email masks" and lets free users create up to 50 of them, with the option to disable or delete a mask at any time. Proton Mail calls them hide-my-email aliases: randomly generated throwaway addresses that forward messages to your mailbox, where the sender never sees your real address. Proton Free and Mail Plus allow up to 10 of these aliases, while higher tiers allow unlimited aliases. [Apple's Hide My Email](/blog/apple-hide-my-email-how-it-works) uses @privaterelay.appleid.com addresses that forward to the verified email on your Apple Account and restricts forwarding to the senders designated for that app or website. You can also use plus-addressing, such as yourname+newsletter@example.com, which requires no setup at all but is trivially guessable because it still contains your real address. The common thread across every alias type is continuity: the address is meant to keep receiving, and you manage it as a standing filter rather than a disposable object.

## How a temporary inbox works

A temporary inbox receives mail directly at a newly created address instead of forwarding it somewhere else. On the service described here, you copy the address, paste it where it is needed, and read the message in the same browser session while the inbox is active. There is no account and no identity check, which also means there is no account to recover: access lives in a private browser cookie, plus a recovery token you should save if you want to reopen the inbox from another device. Because the inbox is temporary by design, message retention is bounded, and the operator's configured window governs how long mail stays readable. Attachments are available only when the operator has enabled a malware scanner; on this deployment they are disabled, so treat the inbox as plain-text-first. Nothing here should be read as a permanent product default. Retention, extensions, outbound sending, and attachment handling are configuration choices an operator can change, and the limits shown in the interface are the ones that actually apply to you.

## When an alias is the better choice

Choose an alias when you expect a relationship to continue. If you are signing up for a service you may need to log into again, if you want order confirmations or statements, or if you may need to receive a future password reset at that address, an alias keeps the door open while still shielding your primary address. Aliases also hold up better the moment you need to reply, because you can typically send from the alias, and if a sender turns out to be abusive you can disable that one alias without changing your real address everywhere. They are also the more mature choice for organizing a mailbox, since many providers let you label or filter mail by alias. The trade-off is dependency. An alias depends on a provider account and a permanent inbox you must keep secure. If that account is compromised or lost, every alias behind it stops delivering, so an alias is not a substitute for protecting the underlying mailbox with strong authentication and a recovery plan you have actually tested.

## When a temporary inbox is the better choice

A temporary inbox is the cleaner fit when the interaction is genuinely one-off and you will not need to return. Downloading a single resource, reading a gated guide, checking whether a newsletter is worth keeping, or receiving a confirmation from a service you are only evaluating are all good examples. It is also a sensible tool for testing an email workflow you own: you can send a message to a throwaway address and confirm what actually arrives without polluting your real inbox. Because there is no registration, there is nothing to delete later, and automatic expiry acts as a backstop if you forget. The configured limits matter here. On this deployment the inbox cannot receive attachments, address creation is rate-limited and may require a security check, and some senders block disposable domains outright. A temporary inbox is also the wrong tool whenever continuity matters: never use one for banking, identity documents, or any account whose password recovery you depend on.

## Privacy limits: neither option is full anonymity

It is tempting to treat both as anonymity tools, and that is the most common mistake. An alias hides your real address from the recipient, but the alias provider can still see the metadata required to route the message. Apple is explicit that it does not read or process the content of messages passing through Hide My Email, apart from the standard spam filtering required to remain a trusted email provider, and that messages are deleted from its relay servers after delivery, usually within seconds. That is a useful transparency statement about one provider's handling, not a universal guarantee. A temporary inbox reduces your exposure differently: you disclose no permanent address, but the website, its mail infrastructure, and your network provider may still process connection data, and anything you type into a form travels to that site no matter which address you used. Remote images can leak your IP address to the image host, which is why blocking them by default is a sensible precaution. Neither approach, on its own, makes you anonymous, and neither currently comes with a security guarantee you can rely on for sensitive data.

## A quick decision checklist

Use the following as a short triage. If the answer to "will I need mail at this address again?" is yes, pick an alias. If it is no, [a temporary inbox](/blog/temporary-email-qa-testing-guide) is simpler. If you need to send replies or manage the address long-term, choose an alias. If the task is a single download or a test you own, choose a temporary inbox. If the service is a bank, a broker, a government portal, or anything tied to recovery of a valuable account, choose neither. Instead use a permanent, strongly authenticated mailbox. If you are unsure whether a site accepts disposable domains, assume it may not and keep a fallback ready. Finally, check the practical limits before you commit: an alias provider's free-tier cap, such as 50 masks on Firefox Relay's free plan or 10 hide-my-email aliases on Proton Free and Mail Plus, or a temporary inbox's configured expiry window. Matching the tool to the expected lifetime of the relationship prevents the most common failure mode, which is discovering too late that the address you used for something important has already expired or been rejected.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I turn a temporary inbox into a permanent one?** No. A temporary inbox is defined by its expiry, and once the configured lifetime ends, the address stops receiving mail and access ends. If you want an address that persists, create an alias or use a permanent mailbox instead of hoping to extend a temporary one indefinitely.

**Does an alias still expose me to spam?** The alias protects your primary address, but spam can still arrive through the alias itself. The advantage is control: you can disable or delete that single alias and stop the stream without changing your real address, which is far less disruptive than abandoning a mailbox.

**Which is better for signing up to something I might use later?** An alias, as long as you understand that it depends on a provider account and an underlying mailbox you keep secure. A temporary inbox is suitable only when you are confident you will never need the address again, including for password recovery.

**Are these approaches safe for financial accounts?** No. Neither is designed for banking, brokerage, or any account tied to identity or recovery. Use a permanent mailbox with strong authentication for anything that holds money or identity data, and treat temporary email tools as short-lived conveniences rather than secure vaults.
