# The Brevo SAML phishing campaign: what happened and how to stay safe

In September 2026, an attacker abused a flaw in the way the email marketing platform Brevo handles SAML single sign-on to reach customer accounts and send phishing email through legitimate infrastructure. Brevo published a write-up on its status page, and the incident is notable because the messages passed normal authentication checks and looked genuine. This article summarizes what Brevo and the affected companies have said, attributes each claim to its source, and explains what the episode teaches about trusting email that arrives from a real domain. It deliberately avoids speculation about details that have not been disclosed. The goal is practical: help you recognize this class of attack and reduce your exposure if your address was on one of the affected lists.

## What Brevo disclosed

Brevo's own incident write-up states that on September 10, 2026, at 6:30 AM UTC, it identified a security issue in which an attacker exploited a flaw in how the platform handles SAML SSO to gain access to 138 Brevo accounts. According to the same write-up, six of those accounts were used to send phishing emails to the contacts stored there, contacts were exported from 43 accounts, and 93 accounts showed no meaningful activity. Brevo says it closed the route the attacker used at 8:30 AM UTC and signed out every user on the platform, and that there has been no further attacker activity since. The company says it is contacting every affected customer directly. Brevo also acknowledges the failure plainly, writing that customers trust it with access to their audiences and that in this case it failed to protect it. Brevo also said it is filing a legal complaint, will cooperate with authorities, and is contacting every affected customer directly with details specific to their account.

## How the attacker gained access

Brevo's explanation is unusually specific and worth following closely. The attacker created a Brevo account and enabled single sign-on on it, then invited legitimate Brevo users into that SSO configuration. Using their own identity provider, the attacker was able to sign in as those invited users, which Brevo notes is by itself expected behavior for SSO. The failure was in scoping: Brevo states that the access was not properly restricted and that instead of being limited to the single organization where SSO was enabled, it wrongly granted the attacker access to all organizations those users could reach. Brevo describes the root cause as a boundary that was not enforced, namely that a login arriving through one company's SSO configuration should only ever reach that company's account. The company says it deployed a permanent fix limiting SSO access to the organization that owns the SSO configuration. Brevo adds that SSO invitations are being re-enabled only once that scoping fix is in place, so the restriction is enforced rather than merely announced.

## Who was affected

Reporting from SecurityWeek and Malwarebytes describes the downstream impact. Trezor, a hardware wallet maker, published a notice saying the incident affected its opt-in newsletter database of roughly 347,000 email addresses and that the phishing email was sent from its Brevo account. Trezor reports that it took the malicious domain down at the DNS level within 20 minutes, limiting access to about 2,500 people who had clicked before the link stopped working. Trezor's notice gives the subject line as "Critical Security Alert: STM32 Entropy Vulnerability." SecurityWeek also notes that BitBox and CoinTracking appear to have been affected, while Malwarebytes reports CoinTracking warned customers about an email titled "Data Breach Notice: Please refresh API Keys as soon as possible." The exact number of people who lost funds is not known from the public statements, and this article will not estimate it. Readers should treat the list of affected companies as what has been publicly confirmed rather than as a complete accounting of every Brevo customer involved.

## Why these messages were hard to spot

The reason this campaign was effective is structural rather than clever wording. Brevo states that the messages were sent through legitimate infrastructure, so they passed the usual email authentication checks and looked genuine. That is the uncomfortable lesson: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC prove that a message came from the domain it claims, and from infrastructure that domain is authorized to use. They do not prove that the sender had good intentions or that the content is true. When an attacker operates inside a legitimate marketing platform, every technical trust signal is correct, and only judgment about the content can catch it. Malwarebytes makes the same point, advising readers to check a company's official website or app when a message claims an urgent security problem, and never to install an app from a link in an unsolicited email. A useful habit is to treat urgency itself as a warning sign, because genuine security notices rarely require immediate action through a link.

## What to do if you received one

If you received a phishing message from an affected sender, the advice is consistent across Trezor, Brevo, and the security coverage. Do not click any links, and if the message is still in your inbox, delete it. Verify any security claim by going directly to the company's official website or app rather than through the email. Never enter a wallet recovery phrase anywhere except the physical device, and never provide recovery phrases, API keys, or login details in response to an email. If you did enter a wallet backup or seed phrase after following a link, Trezor's guidance is to move your funds to a new wallet immediately. Treat contact data exported from the affected accounts as a risk for future targeted phishing, since Malwarebytes notes those addresses could be used in later campaigns. If you are unsure whether a message is genuine, contact the company through a channel you looked up yourself rather than one the message provides.

## What this incident teaches about email trust

Three lessons stand out without speculation. First, a legitimate domain is not proof of a legitimate message, so the habit of verifying urgent security claims through an independent channel is the only reliable defense. Second, third-party senders are a supply-chain risk: an organization can have excellent internal security and still have its audience reached through a compromised marketing provider, which is precisely what Trezor emphasized when it said its own systems were not affected. Third, the export of contact lists from 43 accounts means the fallout outlasts the incident, because a valid email address combined with plausible branding is enough raw material for a convincing follow-up. Brevo said it disabled the links in the affected emails and advised recipients not to click them, which is useful after the fact but does not replace user-side verification. For individuals the defence is procedural rather than technical, and it costs nothing to apply.

## Practical lessons for anyone running email lists

If your organization sends bulk email, this incident is a reminder to treat your sending platform as a high-value system. Scope SSO configurations strictly, and verify that a login authenticated through one organization can never reach another; that boundary is exactly what failed here. Apply least privilege so that marketing and support accounts cannot export the full contact database by default. Monitor for unusual exports and for sending spikes, since the abuse in this case involved sending from six accounts and exporting contacts from 43. Have a tested plan to revoke sessions and disable links when something goes wrong, because Brevo's speed in doing so appears to have limited the damage. Finally, when you notify your audience, say clearly what happened and what you are doing about it, as Trezor did. Review which third-party platforms are able to email your audience, and make sure each one is covered by your incident response plan rather than treated as someone else's problem.

## How temporary email fits, and where it does not

[A temporary address](/blog/temporary-email-vs-email-aliases) reduces the exposure of your primary inbox when you sign up for something new, and it can be a reasonable choice for low-stakes newsletters you are only sampling, subject to the [privacy policy](/privacy) that governs how data is handled. On the service described here, mail is received at an address that expires at the end of the operator's configured window, with a default retention of 60 minutes and a maximum of 1440 minutes on this deployment, and attachments are disabled unless the operator enables a scanner. That is useful for keeping a permanent address out of a marketing database in the first place. It is not a remedy once your address is already on a breached list, and it is emphatically not appropriate for cryptocurrency exchanges, wallets, banking, or any account tied to funds or identity. For those, use a permanent, strongly authenticated mailbox and treat every unsolicited security alert as suspect until you confirm it independently. In short, a disposable address reduces future exposure; it does not repair exposure that has already happened.

## Frequently asked questions

**Did Trezor or the other companies get hacked directly?** According to Trezor's own notice, no. It describes the incident as a breach of Brevo, its third-party newsletter platform, and states that no Trezor product, wallet, or account system was affected.

**Were the phishing emails technically fake?** They were sent through legitimate infrastructure, which is why Brevo says they passed normal email authentication checks. That is what made them convincing; the content was still malicious.

**How many people were affected?** Brevo reports 138 accounts were accessed, six were used to send phishing email, and 43 had contacts exported. Trezor reports that about 347,000 of its newsletter subscribers received a message. The number who lost funds is not publicly established.

**What should I do if I clicked but entered nothing?** Trezor states that clicking the link alone does not expose your funds, and the risk applies if you entered your wallet backup. Even so, stop interacting with the message, delete it, and verify any future security notice directly with the company.
